# A decade of building websites: what changed, what stayed the same

I have been building websites for about ten years now. Not as a hobby — as real work for real clients, from small local businesses to government organisations.

Looking back, I realise the path was not a straight line from "beginner" to "expert." It was a series of detours, hard lessons, and moments where I had to admit I was wrong about something I thought I understood.

This article is the honest version of that journey. What changed, what I stopped doing, and what I still believe after a decade of building for the web.

## The first sites

My first real project was ladycolors.nl. I look back and it is nothing special — a simple site, probably too heavy, built with tools I barely understood. But it was mine. I built it, I learned from it, and it led to the next one.

From there, I worked with a team on deleriumcafeamsterdam.nl, where I learned about SEO and UX from people who knew more than I did. Then came more sites, one after another: hostessbureau.com, ledimpact.nl, ergogooi.nl, fysiodouma.nl. Each one taught me something new about how websites actually work — not just how to build them, but how they perform, how clients use them, and how they need to be maintained.

At that time, I thought I knew what I was doing. In reality, I was learning the basics. But that is how building works: you do not wait until you are ready. You build, you learn, you improve.

## The custom CMS detour

At some point, I wanted more control. [WordPress](/what-is/wordpress) was good, but I wanted something that was built exactly for the projects I was working on.

So I started building moons.io CMS — a Laravel-based custom CMS. The ideas were good: more control, better performance, a system designed for specific use cases. I worked with a colleague who handled much of the development, and I learned my first real steps in PHP and hosting along the way.

But maintaining a custom CMS is a different game than building one. Every new feature took weeks. Every update was a risk. Every bug was mine to fix, with no plugin developer to blame and no community to ask.

The lesson was clear: building your own CMS feels powerful, but the maintenance cost is brutal. I learned that having good ideas is not enough. You need a sustainable way to execute them. Building everything yourself is rarely that way.

## The turning point: a custom WordPress theme

The project that changed how I approached development was robinzon.nl. It was the first time a client asked for a fully custom WordPress theme, designed in Figma, with marketing integrations and a specific user experience.

Until then, I had been using existing themes and modifying them. This project forced me to design from scratch, think about the content model before the layout, and connect the frontend to real marketing tools.

I learned that custom development is not about showing off technical skill. It is about building exactly what the client needs — nothing more, nothing less. That restraint became a principle I still use today.

## The sustainability shift

Around late 2021, something shifted. I was part of a network called BNI Friendship, where I met people doing meaningful work in sustainability. I wanted to apply that thinking to my own work.

I started researching how websites impact the environment. Not as a marketing angle — I genuinely wanted to know whether what I was building was doing more harm than necessary.

Then I met Merel. We worked together on carbonfix.org, a project about measuring and reducing the carbon footprint of websites. That collaboration grew into [Carbonfooter](/what-is/carbonfooter), The Sustainable Theme, and Pixel to Planet. Suddenly, sustainability was not something I thought about on the side. It was becoming the core of how I build.

## The modern web pivot

A couple of years ago, a friend introduced me to [Next.js](/what-is/nextjs) and [Payload CMS](/what-is/payload-cms). He was a better developer than me, and I trusted his judgment. We did a few coding sessions together, and I started reading about data freedom, flexibility, and security.

The more I explored, the more I saw possibilities that WordPress did not offer: content as structured data, TypeScript from database to frontend, pre-rendered pages by default, and the freedom to design without guardrails.

My first real project with this stack was my own site, moonsio.nl. Building for yourself gives you room to experiment, but it is still a real project. After that came fysiodouma.nl (a rebuild), and later more complex projects like duizeligheid.nl.

I did not abandon [WordPress](/what-is/wordpress). I still use it for projects where it is the better choice. But I added new tools, and I changed how I think about content, architecture, and performance.

## What stayed the same

After ten years, some things have not changed.

I still want the best result for every client. I still want to optimise everything. I still push for quality, even when it is easier to settle.

But what changed is how I define "best." It used to mean the most features, the most control, the most technology. Now it means something more specific: a technically solid website with good design and UX, that emits less than 0.05 g of CO₂ per visit, is easy to maintain, converts well, keeps the client happy, and does not hurt the planet. That is a much harder target than "make it look good and work." But it is a better one.

I have also learned that rebuilding everything from scratch is not scalable. It is not sustainable. The drive to optimise is good, but applying it by rebuilding every time you learn something new is not. I am still learning to balance the desire for perfect with the need for practical.

## What I know now

If I could go back and tell my younger self a few things, it would be this:

- Build for maintainability first. The most impressive code is useless if no one can update it in two years.
- Do not build what you can use. Custom CMS projects feel like progress, but they often create more problems than they solve.
- Sustainability is not a feature you add later. It is a quality of the decisions you make from the start.
- Stay curious. The moment you stop exploring is the moment you stop knowing whether your default is still the right choice.
- Do not get lost in rebuilding. Learning something new does not mean you have to rebuild everything you already built. Apply new knowledge to new projects, and let the old ones live.

## The next decade

I do not know exactly what the next ten years will look like. But I know the direction: less waste, better judgment, and software that is built to last — not just to launch.

I will probably look back at this article in a few years and smile at what I did not know yet. That is fine. That is how building works.
